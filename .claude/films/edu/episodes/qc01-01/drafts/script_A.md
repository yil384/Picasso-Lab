# QC01-01 "From Two Qubits to a Thousand" - script draft A (story / analogy first)

CSE 291P Quantum Computing Systems, Prof. Yufei Ding, UC San Diego, 2025 Lecture 1, slide p8 "Why Now? Faster than
Expected Hardware Development", recording 11:32-19:10. Vertical 1080x1920, English, target 5:15.

Angle: one everyday picture carries the idea. Two invented characters, an old physicist and a young computer-science
student, and a lab door. Locked (lab access) -> a mail slot (cloud access) -> behind it, five kinds of machine ->
the door held open (why computer scientists now). Every number hangs on the door story; the taped slide is where
the numbers are checked and corrected. Warm, plain, no winks: the gags (the mail slot, the parka, the student still
counting, the undecided walk past the stalls) are each the explanation of that beat.

Look: warm paper, black ink drawings that draw themselves, red pen corrects and emphasises, watercolour washes
(blue for the cold, yellow for light / the open door). Her slide p8 taped on, the logo legend covered by a blank taped
sheet. Her clips: mic tag "Prof. Ding, lecture 1", red-bordered captions. She is never drawn.

## Narration and clips, in order
Facts rows used are in brackets (FACTS.md, qc01-01). Clips are her exact words and are never repeated by the narrator.

n0: Two qubits on a lab bench. Is that even a computer?  [F2, F4]
n1: For seventeen years, quantum computers stayed about that small: 2 qubits in 1998, 12 at most.  [F1]
n2: Within the next nine years, one chip held over a thousand. What changed?  [F6, F7]
n3: Meet two people we made up: an old physicist and a young computer-science student.  [-]
n4: A qubit is a quantum bit, the unit a quantum computer works with.  [definition]
n5: In 1998, the physicist's machines had two of them: molecules in a liquid, read by magnets.  [F2, F3]
n6: They ran tiny demos, like finding one item out of four in a single look.  [F3]
n7: It works. So, back to the question: is it a computer?  [F4 set-up]
C4 (6.0 s): "you can only verify the type of gates on it, and it's not really that useful."  [F4]
n8: Her slide calls this stretch the past seventeen years, 1998 to 2015.  [F1]
n9: The machines grew slowly: two qubits, then seven in 2000, then twelve in 2006.  [F1]
n10: The slide's "7 to 12" counts the biggest ones, so the red pen adds the twos.  [F1, slide erratum]
n11: And under the numbers, two words: lab access.  [F5]
n12: For our student, that meant a locked door.  [F5]
n13: The machine lived in one lab, and only the lab that built it could use it.  [F5]
C5 (8.2 s): "unless you are physics students and your lab is doing quantum computing and building quantum hardware you will never have access to this that kind of computer"  [F5]
n14: So a computer-science student could only stand on tiptoe and look through the glass.  [F5]
n15: Then, in the recent nine years, the door began to give.  [F6]
C1 (5.5 s): "quantum hardware is really developing very fast, faster than expected"  [F26]
n16: In May 2016, IBM put a five-qubit quantum computer online for anyone to try.  [F11]
n17: That is cloud access: the machine stays in its cold room, and you send it a program from your laptop over the internet.  [F11, F19]
n18: The door was still shut, but now it had a mail slot. Programs in, answers out.  [F11]
n19: Meanwhile, the chips kept growing.  [F9]
n20: 127 qubits in 2021. 433 in 2022, the number on her 2024 slide.  [F9, F8]
n21: A year later, her slide said over a thousand.  [F8]
C12 (5.7 s): "not only we have much larger quantum computers and up to like 1,000 qubits"  [F12, F7]
n22: To be exact, IBM's Condor chip, December 2023, held 1,121 qubits. The red pen fixes one digit on the slide.  [F7, slide erratum]
n23: Our physicist holds two. The student is still counting.  [F2, F7]
n24: One honest catch: that 1,121-qubit chip was a research test.  [F12]
n25: The machines people run code on today have about 100 to 160 qubits, as of October 2026.  [F12]
n26: More qubits only help if they make few mistakes.  [F13]
n27: After the big chip, IBM put a smaller chip with fewer errors online.  [F13]
n28: And behind the door, there was no longer one kind of machine. There were five.  [F15]
n29: Superconducting circuits on a chip.  [F15, F19]
n30: Ions held in a row in a vacuum.  [F15, F23]
C11 (5.8 s): "ion trap is a very mature technology that has been used to build quantum computers"  [F17]
n31: Neutral atoms in a grid, held by beams of light.  [F15, F23]
n32: Photons, particles of light; their builders say light is easier to scale.  [F15, F18]
n33: And silicon spins, where each qubit is almost a transistor, so it can be made in chip factories.  [F15, F16]
n34: Five kinds, no winner yet. The student can't pick a stall.  [F15]
n35: One big difference between the stalls is the cold.  [F19, F22]
n36: A superconducting chip is kept about a hundredth of a degree above absolute zero, over 100 times colder than deep space.  [F19, F20]
n37: The metal only superconducts when it is cold. Hence the parka.  [F21]
n38: And the others?  [F22 set-up]
C9 (5.4 s): "So generally speaking, in principle, only superconducting requires low temperature."  [F22]
n39: Ions and atoms float in a vacuum and are chilled by lasers, so the box itself can stay at room temperature.  [F23]
n40: Light doesn't mind the heat, but the detectors that count single photons are kept very cold.  [F24]
C10 (4.7 s): "But sometimes we still use low temperature to get better results."  [F22, F23]
n41: Her slide ends with one more arrow: the future six years, to a million qubits.  [F14]
n42: Company roadmaps do aim at about a million. Plans, not machines: one plans 2 million by 2030; others give no date.  [F14]
n43: So here is what changed: real machines, many kinds, all online.  [F12, F15, F25]
n44: Now they need people who write and compile programs for them.  [F25]
n45: Which is why the physicist is holding the door open, and the student walks in, laptop under one arm.  [F25]
n46: Next time: what has been done before.  [her next topic]
