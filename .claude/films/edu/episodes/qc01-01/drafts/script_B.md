## Narration (n = synthetic narrator; c = her clip, mic tag "Prof. Ding, lecture 1", red-bordered caption)

**P0 cover / hook**
- n0: For seventeen years, a quantum computer meant two qubits on a lab bench. (F1, F2)
- n1: Nine years later, a chip held over a thousand. (F6, F7)
- n2: Is a two-qubit machine even a computer? And what changed? (hook; answered by C4 and n44-n46)

**P1 the chart**
- n3: First, a qubit is a quantum bit, the unit a quantum computer works with. (definition)
- n4: This is the chart Professor Yufei Ding uses to open her course on quantum computing systems at UC San Diego. (DING25 p8)
- n5: Look at its title. (F26)
- c1: "quantum hardware is really developing very fast, faster than expected" (5.5 s; F26)
- n6: The chart cuts time into three stretches: seventeen years, nine years, six years. (F1, F6, F14)
- n7: Let's walk it, left to right, with a red pen. (-)

**P2 stretch one: the bench**
- n8: Stretch one runs from 1998 to 2015. (F1)
- n9: In 1998, the machines had just 2 qubits: molecules in a liquid, read by magnets. (F2, F3)
- n10: The slide says 7 to 12; those are the biggest ones, 7 in 2000 and 12 in 2006. So the pen writes 2 to 12. (F1, slide erratum)
- n11: A two-qubit machine ran tiny demos, like finding one item out of 4 in a single look. (F3)
- n12: So is that a computer? (F4, set-up)
- c4: "you can only verify the type of gates on it, and it's not really that useful." (6.0 s; F4)
- n13: Enough for a demo, not for real work. (F4)

**P3 lab access**
- n14: The last words over this stretch are "Lab Access". (F5, DING25 p8)
- c5: "unless you are physics students and your lab is doing quantum computing and building quantum hardware you will never have access to this that kind of computer" (8.2 s; F5)
- n15: Only the lab that built it could use it. Everyone else waited outside the door. (F5)

**P4 stretch two: the count**
- n16: Stretch two: the nine years after 2015. (F6)
- n17: Along the orange band the count climbs: 127 qubits in 2021, 433 in 2022. (F9, F8)
- n18: Her 2024 slide said 433. A year later, it said over a thousand. (F8)
- n19: The red pen fixes one digit here: IBM's chip from December 2023 had 1,121 qubits. (F7, slide erratum; the voice never says the slide's number)
- c12: "not only we have much larger quantum computers and up to like 1,000 qubits" (5.7 s; F12, softened by n23-n26)

**P5 cloud access**
- n20: And under the count, two new words: "Cloud Access". (DING25 p8, F11)
- n21: Cloud access means the machine stays in the builder's lab; you send your program over the internet, and the results come back. (F11, plain definition)
- n22: In May 2016, IBM put a 5-qubit quantum computer online for anyone to try. (F11; never "first")
- n23: The locked door became a login page. (F5, F11)
- n24: One honest limit: the 1,121-qubit chip was a research test. (F12)
- n25: The machines people run code on today have about 100 to 160 qubits, as of October 2026. (F12)
- n26: More qubits only help if they make few mistakes. After the big chip, IBM put a smaller chip with fewer errors online. (F13)

**P6 stretch three: plans**
- n27: Stretch three: the next six years, with arrows up to a million qubits. (F14, DING25 p8)
- n28: The red pen adds one word: plans. (F14)
- n29: Company roadmaps aim at about a million qubits. One plans 2 million by 2030; others give no date. (F14)
- n30: Plans, not machines. (F14)

**P7 the bands**
- n31: Now look at the colours. The arrows are not one line but several bands. (DING25 p8)
- n32: Each band is a different way to build a qubit. (F15)
- n33: Orange is superconducting circuits: metal on a chip. (F15, F21)
- n34: Blue is trapped ions, and grey is neutral atoms: single atoms held in place and chilled by lasers. (F15, F23)
- n35: Green is photons, particles of light; their builders say light is easier to scale. (F15, F18)
- n36: And one kind shows up only as a lone dot. (F15)
- c13: "Intel is using quantum dots," (3.0 s; F15, F16)
- n37: Each of those qubits is almost a transistor, so it can be made in chip factories. (F16)
- n38: Five kinds, no winner yet. (F15)

**P8 the cold question**
- n39: Which raises the question: does every quantum computer live in a freezer? (F19-F24, set-up)
- n40: The superconducting ones do: about a hundredth of a degree above absolute zero, over 100 times colder than deep space. (F19, F20)
- n41: The metal only superconducts when it is cold. And the others? (F21, set-up for C9)
- c9: "So generally speaking, in principle, only superconducting requires low temperature." (5.4 s; F22)
- c10: "But sometimes we still use low temperature to get better results." (4.7 s; F22)
- n42: Ions and atoms float in a vacuum, chilled by lasers. The box can stay at room temperature, but some machines cool it anyway for a cleaner vacuum. (F23)
- n43: And light doesn't mind the heat, but the detectors that count single photons are kept very cold. (F24, the honest limit to "in principle")

**P9 open door + next**
- n44: So what changed? Real machines, many kinds, all online. (F25)
- n45: Now they need people who write and compile programs for them: computer scientists. (F25, series framing, not a quote)
- n46: The lab door is open. (F25)
- n47: Next: what has been done before. (tease; her next topic is the history of the field)
